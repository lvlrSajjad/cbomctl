# Signatures aren't urgent — but they aren't simple

The standard advice on post-quantum migration is to do key exchange first and
signatures later. That advice is correct, it is well sourced, and it leads
people to a conclusion that is wrong.

## Why key exchange comes first

An adversary who records your TLS traffic today can decrypt it the day a
cryptanalytically relevant quantum computer exists. The data is already
captured; the only missing ingredient is the machine. BSI names it directly:

> "encrypted data can already be stored for later decryption ('Store Now,
> Decrypt Later')"

Signatures do not work that way. Forging one requires the quantum computer to
exist *at the moment of forgery*. Nothing you sign today becomes forgeable
retroactively. BSI again, in the same section:

> "In contrast to key agreement, classic signatures are still trustworthy as
> long as no cryptographically relevant quantum computer exists."

The deadlines follow from that, and the gap is visible in the documents. BSI
puts classical key agreement at 2031 and classical signatures at 2035. US
Executive Order 14412 puts federal high value assets on post-quantum key
establishment by 31 December 2030 and post-quantum **signatures by 31 December
2031** — one order, one population, consecutive clauses in a single sentence,
one year apart. The order gives no reason for the gap. The obvious reading is
harvest exposure, but that inference is mine, not the document's.

The exception is signatures whose *verification* is long-lived. Firmware, code
signing, document signing, root CAs: a signature issued today is still being
trusted in 2040, so the relevant horizon is not how long the data stays secret
but how long the signature stays believed. The EU roadmap makes the point
explicitly, asking that "the upgrade mechanism for software and firmware
upgrades should incorporate post-quantum signature schemes for integrity and
authenticity."

So far, so orderly. Key exchange urgent, signatures later, firmware special.

## The part that breaks the tidy version

Ask *what to migrate signatures to*, and the calm evaporates.

ANSSI recommends that post-quantum signatures be deployed alongside a classical
signature — the same hybrid construction it wants for key establishment. So does
BSI: its 2026-01 guideline "recommends the use of a quantum-safe signature
scheme **only in combination with a classic signature scheme**."

The reason has nothing to do with quantum computers. ANSSI states it:

> "even if the post-quantum algorithms have gained a lot of attention, they are
> still not mature enough to solely ensure the security. For example, several
> post-quantum schemes have suffered from classical attacks in the past years"

The reference on that sentence is Beullens, *Breaking Rainbow takes a weekend on
a laptop*. Rainbow was a NIST finalist. SIKE was broken in the fourth round. The
worry is not Shor — it is that ML-DSA is young, and young schemes sometimes turn
out to be wrong. That risk does not shrink as 2035 approaches. It is orthogonal
to the timeline entirely.

Which produces the situation this article is named for. A TLS handshake
signature is genuinely **low urgency** — verified once, discarded in
milliseconds, nothing to harvest — and under BSI and ANSSI it may still need a
hybrid target, for reasons that have nothing to do with when you get to it.

Two questions, not one:

| | question | driven by | reaches |
|---|---|---|---|
| **urgency** | when must I move? | harvest exposure × data lifetime | key exchange; signatures only when verification is long-lived |
| **assurance** | what must I move *to*? | maturity of the target scheme | everything, signatures included |

Most migration plans I have seen answer the first and never ask the second.

## The carve-out that shows they are reasoning, not rule-making

If "hybrid everything" were a slogan, it would apply to all post-quantum
signatures. It does not.

BSI and ANSSI both **exempt hash-based signatures** — SLH-DSA, XMSS, LMS — from
the hybrid recommendation. BSI:

> "hash-based signatures can, provided that the implementation security of
> stateful and stateless hash-based mechanisms is carefully considered, in
> principle also be used alone (i.e. not in hybrid form)"

ANSSI reaches the same exception independently, permitting standalone
hash-based signatures where hybridisation is otherwise mandatory for its
security visas.

The logic is consistent: hash-based signature security rests only on assumptions
about hash functions, which are old, heavily studied, and not the thing that
broke in Rainbow. There is no young lattice assumption to hedge against, so
there is nothing for a classical algorithm to hedge. The maturity argument,
applied rather than recited.

Worth flagging, because this is an article about checking things: a
widely-circulated secondary summary states BSI wants hybrid for *all* post-quantum
algorithms including hash-based ones. The primary text says the opposite.

And the third position on the same family: the NSA's CNSA 2.0 FAQ answers "Can I
use SLH-DSA to sign software?" with *"While SLH-DSA is hash-based, it is not part
of CNSA and is not approved for any use in NSS."* Approved standalone in Germany
and France, not approved at all for US national security systems — while LMS and
XMSS *are* approved there, but only for firmware and software signing, and HSS
and XMSS^MT "are not allowed."

Three signing assets, and the interesting row is the middle one. `SLH-DSA` is
`INFO` under BSI and ANSSI — they have an explicit rule *permitting* it
standalone — `PASS` under ASD, and `FAIL` under CNSA 2.0, which does not approve
it. Same algorithm, three answers, none of them about timing.

Meanwhile `ML-DSA-65` draws a construction conflict (Europe wants it hybrid, the
NSA does not permit that) *and* a parameter conflict (ASD and the NSA both want
ML-DSA-87). Neither is an urgency finding. Both are assurance findings, on an
asset that is not urgent at all.

## Try it

```bash
pip install cbomctl
cbomctl verdict your-cbom.json -j bsi-de,anssi-fr,asd-au,cnsa-2.0
```

Every rule cites the document, the section or page, and the date it was read.
`cbomctl prioritize` answers the urgency question separately, using data
lifetimes you supply — because no CBOM knows how long your data has to stay
secret, and that is the input the whole ranking depends on.

The one thing it will not do is guess. If your CBOM does not say what a key is
for — and in real CBOMkit output, 8 of 22 algorithm components do not — it says
so, and tells you what the answer would change.
